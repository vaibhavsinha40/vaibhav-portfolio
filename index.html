Business case / 01

How Can Uber Prevent Surge Pricing Manipulation?

What if drivers deliberately go offline to increase ride fares? How can Uber distinguish genuine shortages from artificial ones?

I explored this problem from a Business Analysis, Product Thinking, and Data Analytics perspective.

This is a hypothetical case study.

1. The business problem

Uber uses Surge Pricing, where fares increase when passenger demand exceeds driver availability.

Imagine this scenario at an airport:

  • 500 drivers are physically present.
  • 400 drivers deliberately go offline.
  • The algorithm detects low supply and increases fares.
  • Drivers return online and accept higher-fare rides.

The result? Artificial shortages, unfair pricing, reduced customer trust, and potential system manipulation.

2. Solution 1: Airport Virtual Queue (FIFO)

Implement a First-In, First-Out queue at airport waiting areas.

  • Every driver receives a queue position.
  • Going offline deliberately means losing that position.
  • Returning drivers rejoin at the end of the queue.

Business Impact: Discourages intentional offline activity and promotes fair ride allocation, with exceptions for genuine technical issues.

3. Solution 2: Real-time anomaly detection

Consider this driver activity:

Time Driver activity
10:00 AM 420 drivers online
10:10 AM Only 20 drivers online
10:15 AM Surge pricing increases
10:20 AM 400 drivers return online

An anomaly detection system could monitor:

  • Simultaneous offline activity
  • Offline duration and sudden reactivation
  • Airport location and historical patterns
  • Surge pricing movements

Using these factors, the system generates an Anomaly Score to flag suspicious behavior and trigger pricing safeguards.

Business Impact: Helps distinguish genuine shortages from potential manipulation.

4. Key KPIs to measure success

As a Business Analyst, I would track:

  • Suspicious Offline Events — Unusual driver activity
  • Surge Pricing Frequency — Rides affected by surge pricing
  • Driver Availability Rate — Percentage of eligible drivers online
  • Passenger Waiting Time — Average wait for a ride
  • False Positive Rate — Normal activity incorrectly flagged

5. Final recommendation

Combine both approaches:

  • Virtual Queue → Discourages manipulation of driver availability.
  • Anomaly Detection → Identifies suspicious patterns and improves pricing decisions.

Together, they could improve pricing fairness, customer trust, fraud prevention, and operational efficiency.

My key takeaway

Great business solutions aren't just about fixing algorithms. They're about understanding user behaviour, identifying loopholes, improving processes, and measuring business impact.

Guesstimate / 04

Estimating the Toothpaste Market Size in India

Bain & Company interview-style market-sizing case

How would you estimate the size of a market without having access to actual market data?

This is a classic market-sizing / guesstimate case often used in consulting interviews.

I tried solving it by breaking the problem into simple, logical assumptions:

Assumption Estimate
India's population ~1.4 billion
Toothpaste penetration ~80%
Toothpaste users ~1.1 billion
Brushing frequency ~1.2 times/day
Usage ~1 gram per brushing
Average tube size ~100 grams
Average price ~₹50/tube

Using these assumptions

1.1B users → ~5B tubes/year → ₹250B market

Estimated Indian toothpaste market: ~₹25,000 crore (~$3B) annually.

The key learning for me wasn't just arriving at the final number. It was learning how to structure an ambiguous problem, make reasonable assumptions, perform quick calculations, and arrive at a defensible estimate.

That's exactly the kind of structured thinking I want to build throughout this 30-day Business Analyst challenge.

In a guesstimate, the exact number matters less than the logic and structure behind the number.

Guesstimate / 05

How Many Diapers Are Sold in Mumbai in a Month?

Estimate the number of diapers sold in Mumbai in a month.

Sounds random, right? But this was actually a guesstimate question I faced in a recent P&G interview.

The interviewer wasn’t looking for an exact number. They wanted to understand how I break down an ambiguous business problem into logical assumptions.

Here’s how I approached it

1. Mumbai population
≈ 20 million people
2. Target population
Assume ~6% are children aged 0–3 years.
20M × 6% = 1.2M babies
3. Diaper adoption
Assume ~70% regularly use disposable diapers.
1.2M × 70% = 840K diaper-using babies
4. Average daily usage
Usage varies by age:
0–6 months → ~6/day
6–12 months → ~4/day
1–3 years → ~2/day
For simplicity, assume an average of 3 diapers per baby per day.
5. Monthly consumption
840K × 3 × 30 ≈ 75.6M diapers/month
6. Adjust for occasional usage
Not every family uses diapers throughout the day. Some use them only at night, while travelling, etc.
Assuming ~80% effective usage:
75.6M × 80% ≈ 60M diapers/month

Final estimate: ~60 million diapers sold per month in Mumbai.

The exact number isn't the point. The real skill being tested is:

Problem → Assumptions → Structured Breakdown → Calculation → Sanity Check → Conclusion

In guesstimate questions, how you think matters more than whether your final number is perfectly accurate.

An interview estimate based on stated assumptions, not actual sales data.
Interview business case / 06

Demand Planning for Ariel Detergent

I was asked this business case study in my recent P&G interview:

You are a Demand Planner. Based on the previous year's sales of Ariel detergent, how would you predict next year's sales, and how would you fulfill the demand?

At first, it sounds like a simple forecasting question. But the real challenge is to think beyond just applying a growth percentage.

Here’s how I would approach it:

1. Analyze Historical Data

Look at 2–3 years of sales data across SKUs, regions, channels, and time periods.

2. Identify Patterns

Analyze trends, seasonality, promotions, stock-outs, and abnormal sales.

3. Build a Forecast

Use appropriate methods such as Moving Average, Weighted Moving Average, Exponential Smoothing, Holt-Winters, or Regression.

4. Add Business Inputs

Incorporate insights from Sales, Marketing, Finance, competitor activity, pricing, promotions, and distribution plans.

5. Plan Supply

Compare forecasted demand with inventory, production capacity, lead time, and existing supply.

6. Maintain Safety Stock

Set appropriate safety stock based on demand variability, lead time, and required service levels.

7. Monitor & Reforecast

Continuously compare actual sales vs. forecast and adjust the forecast when significant deviations occur.

My key takeaway

Demand Planning is not just about predicting sales. It is about understanding the underlying demand and ensuring the right product is available at the right place and right time, while keeping inventory optimized.

This case study was a great reminder that Business Analysts need to combine data-driven thinking with business understanding.
